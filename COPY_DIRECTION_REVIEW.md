# Portfolio Copy Direction Review

Status: Working copy for Carl's review. Nothing in this document is approved public copy.

Review date: August 25, 2026

## What the copy review found

Three independent copy reviews agreed that the first mock sounded too abstract, too symmetrical, and too eager to sound strategic. Phrases such as “Systems that prepare,” “Ambiguity made inspectable,” and “Technical work with expression” describe an attitude without giving the reader anything concrete to picture.

The failure pattern was consistent:

- abstract nouns standing in for an actual idea;
- slogans that could belong to almost any senior engineer;
- evenly balanced verb lists and polished three-part cadence;
- responsible-AI language asserted without naming the safeguard;
- technical range described as categories instead of recognizable work;
- internal product-strategy language leaking into public copy.

## Recommended voice

Carl should sound like a senior builder explaining the work at a whiteboard: specific, candid, technically literate, opinionated, and occasionally wry.

Confidence comes from real nouns, decisions, and boundaries—not adjectives. The copy should say what Carl made, what his part was, what made it difficult, what decision he made, how the system works, and what the evidence can honestly support.

First-person writing is the default. Third-person praise stays visibly attributed.

## Candidate voice territories

### A — Plainspoken proof

Direct, skeptical of hype, and organized around work that can be inspected.

Candidate hero:

> Complicated software should still make sense.

Candidate support:

> I'm Carl Welch. I build AI tools, interactive systems, and audio software. This site shows what I made, how it works, and where the hard decisions were.

Candidate action:

> See the decisions behind the work →

### B — The workbench

Warmer and more personal, with the voice of an experienced maker.

Candidate hero:

> Bring me the knotty part.

Candidate support:

> I work where product questions, technical constraints, and real people collide. The goal is software that makes sense—and keeps making sense once somebody uses it.

Candidate action:

> Come behind the scenes →

### C — Decisions in the open

Editorial and reflective. The portfolio becomes a record of judgment rather than a gallery of finished screens.

Candidate hero:

> Software is a chain of decisions. Here are mine.

Candidate support:

> Applied AI, product systems, and creative software—showing what shaped each decision and what the finished work can honestly prove.

Candidate action:

> Follow the reasoning →

## Recommended starting direction

Begin with **Plainspoken proof**, then borrow the decision-led structure from **Decisions in the open**. It is the clearest match for the engineered visual system and the least likely to drift into portfolio theater.

Current prototype candidates:

- Applied AI: **AI that shows its work.**
- Product systems: **Make the hard parts visible.**
- Creative software: **Code can have a point of view.**
- Recommendations: **From people I've worked with.**

These are layout candidates, not approved final copy. Each supporting paragraph still needs named project evidence.

## Copy-agent workflow

1. **Evidence researcher** prepares a source packet for the page and identifies uncertainty.
2. **Positioning writer** creates materially different options using the approved claim set and Carl's language bank.
3. **Human-voice editor** removes abstraction, canned cadence, generic portfolio language, and synthetic polish.
4. **Hostile hiring-manager editor** asks what each sentence means, where the proof is, and what Carl specifically did.
5. **Claims red-team** checks every factual sentence against its source and restores status or ownership boundaries lost during editing.
6. **Carl** reads the copy in context, revises it in his own words, and approves it before publication.

Agent consensus is not publication approval.

## Required source packet

Every page brief includes:

- the visitor's question;
- one intended takeaway;
- approved claims and evidence references;
- Carl's actual role and contribution;
- project or product status;
- important limits and unsupported inferences;
- phrases taken from Carl's reviewed language bank;
- phrases or implications that are prohibited;
- target reading time and next action.

LinkedIn recommendations are evidence about Carl, not a substitute for Carl's voice. Quotes remain exact and visibly attributed.

## Anti-slop rules

1. If a sentence could appear unchanged on 500 other portfolios, it fails.
2. If Carl would feel ridiculous saying it aloud to another engineer, rewrite it.
3. Every major claim sits beside a named project, employer, decision, result, or attributed recommendation.
4. Prefer objects people can picture: maps, approval gates, security consoles, audio plug-ins, evidence, timelines, MIDI, and rendering paths.
5. Do not say a system is trustworthy when the copy can name the safeguard that earns trust.
6. One sentence makes one claim.
7. Ownership verbs are not interchangeable: designed, implemented, led, contributed, and collaborated each mean something different.
8. Live product, active build, pre-release work, prototype, and planning repository remain visibly distinct.
9. Avoid ornamental abstractions, adjective piles, repeated em dashes, slogan/body repetition, and perfect three-part cadence.
10. Avoid “leveraging,” “unlocking,” “seamless,” “innovative solutions,” “at the intersection of,” “passionate about,” “impactful,” “cutting-edge,” “agentic,” and “end-to-end” unless a specific, deliberate use survives editorial review.
11. Technical terms stay only when they clarify the work.
12. Preserve some edge. Copy polished until nobody could object usually sounds like nobody wrote it.

## Copy acceptance criteria

- Every factual sentence maps to at least one reviewed evidence source.
- Every headline contains a concrete action, object, tension, or point of view.
- Each major section includes at least one detail unique to Carl's work.
- A recruiter can understand the point without decoding a metaphor.
- An engineer can reach the architecture, tradeoff, or validation behind the claim.
- Third-party praise is attributed and never converted into Carl's first-person factual claim.
- Shortened card, mobile, metadata, and social-preview copy preserve the same factual boundaries.
- Placeholder language never appears in a design-approval or publication build.
- Carl reads the final copy aloud and agrees that it sounds like something he would actually say.
